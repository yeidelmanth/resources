It’s Asara B’Teves. Everyone bends over their siddurim, saying selichos in low, serious voices. Line by line, they remember the tragedies that happened to the Yidden in the month of Teves.

Soon, they reach the words: “ּוֶמֶֶלְֶך ָיָָוָן ִאְִנְַסִַנִי ִלְִכְֹּתֹב ָדָת ְיְָוִָנִית—And the Greek king forced me to write the Torah in Greek.”

When was the Torah translated to Greek? And why is that sad enough to mention in selichos?

\*\*\* More than two thousand years ago, the powerful King Talmai (Ptolemy) ruled over Mitzrayim and Yavan (Greece).

He had wealth, power, and a vast empire. But what he loved most was his enormous library, filled with books and scrolls from around the world.

“I want the Torah in my library,” King Talmai declared. “But it’s in Hebrew, and I can’t understand it. I must have it translated into Greek!”

When a king gives an order, no one dares to refuse. “Guards!” he commanded. “Bring me seventy-two of the greatest Jewish sages!”

The officers hurried to obey. Soon, seventy-two talmidei chachamim stood before the king.

But King Talmai didn’t tell them why they had been brought there. Before they could speak to one another, he sent each talmid chacham into a separate room. Then, going from room to room, he gave them all the same command: “Translate the Torah into Greek!”

The Seventy-Two Rooms 8 Teves, 3515 (Just over 100 years before the Chanuka miracle)

But soon, they all realized the same problem: In thirteen places, a correct translation could cause King Talmai to misunderstand the Torah. They would have to change the words to express the Torah's true meaning.

But what if each talmid chacham made different changes? What if the translations didn’t match? King Talmai would be furious!

There was no way to know what the others were writing. They each had to decide on their own.

Finally, on Ches Teves, the translations were finished.

“Lay out the scrolls and compare them!” King Talmai ordered.

The guards obeyed. To the chachamim's amazement, every translation was identical. All seventy-two chachamim had made the exact same thirteen changes!

But surprisingly, the chachamim didn't celebrate. They saw this as one of the saddest days in Jewish history—as tragic as the day the Yidden made the Eigel Hazahav.

Chazal didn’t compare the translation to bowing to the Eigel. They compared it to the day that the Eigel was made.

On that day, no one had worshipped it yet. But what had been created? The possibility for the aveira to happen later.

The same was true on Ches Teves. The chachamim had translated the Torah carefully, and Hashem even made a neis to protect them.

But still, they saw what could happen later. Now that the Torah was available in Greek, anyone could learn it anywhere. Instead of being taught in an environment of warmth and kedusha by teachers with Yiras Shamayim, it could be misunderstood and even misused. This possibility is what made Ches Teves such a sad day.

We can learn from this, too. Sometimes, things seem fine at first—a new book, an exciting game, a funny comment.

But a Yid stops and thinks: Where could this lead? Will it still be good later?
