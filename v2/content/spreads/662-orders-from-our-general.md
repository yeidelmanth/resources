<p class="lead">In this week’s parsha, the Torah says that Hashem fed the Yidden “honey from a rock, and oil from a stone.” That sounds surprising. A rock doesn’t look like something that can give food—certainly not sweet honey or rich oil. And yet, that’s exactly what happened!</p>

You see, when the Yidden were in Mitzrayim, some parents worried about how their children would survive the difficult Galus. But Hashem showed them that there was nothing to fear. Even when the children were surrounded by just plain stones, Hashem turned them into sources of sweetness and strength. Not only did He give the children the basics that they needed to stay alive; He gave them the very best.

This shows how much Hashem loves Yiddishe children.

From the very first Galus, He has taken personal care of them, making sure they have everything they need both b’gashmiyus and b’ruchniyus. And because it’s the children who recognized Hashem’s care so clearly while in Mitzrayim, they were also the first to recognize Him at the Yam Suf— with singing, dancing, and thanking Hashem for his miracles.

Which choice shows real trust in Hashem?

I try hard, but still worry because I don’t see how things will work out.

I do what Hashem wants and trust that He’s taking care of me.

He let them suck honey from a rock, and oil from the mighty part of the stone.

Today, too, Hashem takes care of every single Yiddishe boy and girl. When you remember that Hashem personally gives you everything you need b’gashmiyus and b’ruchniyus, your Avodas Hashem can feel entirely different! It becomes much easier to do what He asks of you, for He has already given you all the tools you need! And you can fulfill your mission with great simcha, knowing that He is focusing on you to complete His Grand Plan!

This simcha brings even more goodness into the world and helps bring closer the final Geulah.

Why did Hashem give the children more than just the basics?

How can remembering Hashem’s care make mitzvos feel happier?

When have you seen something good come from a situation that, at first, appeared difficult or impossible?

Go around the table taking turns. The first person shares one thing that happened during the week that felt like a rock—something hard, plain, and lifeless. Then the next person shares a way to turn that situation into something that can produce honey—the sweetest possible outcome! For example, one sibling might say “this week I got a low mark on my Chumash test,” and the next person might say, “That test might be a chance for you to start learning chumash with tatty every night.” Keep going around until everyone has had a chance to bring “honey” out of a “rock.”.

Yiddishe kinder have a special connection to Hashem, and that’s why Hashem takes such good care of them. Standing by the Yam Suf, they were the ones to feel Him first. Can you find 10 sparks of holiness in places that other people might not see Him?

“Well, how do you do kapparos?” Reb Elimelech replied.

Reb Elimelech nodded. “That’s exactly what I do.”

But the Chassid wasn’t satisfied. “Rebbe, for more than twenty years, I’ve come to spend Aseres Yemei Teshuva with the Rebbe. But I’ve never seen the Rebbe do kapparos. I know it must be very special.”

Reb Elimelach looked at him. “You want to see something special? Then go watch how Moshe the Innkeeper does kapparos.”

The Chassid left right away.

<span class="mc" style="color:#64a950">The Most Special Kapparos</span>

He traveled to Moshe’s inn at the edge of a nearby town and asked if he could stay for the night.

“I’m sorry, but we don’t have any available beds,” Moshe replied. “You can stay at the inn down the road.”

“Please,” the Chassid begged. “I’m very tired. I can rest in a corner.”

Moshe hesitated, then nodded. “Alright,” he said, letting the Chassid in.

The room inside was filled with Goyishe peasants drinking mashke. But after some time, Moshe sent everyone away for the night. At last, the room was empty.

The Chassid lay down under one of the tables and waited to see what would happen.

Before morning, Moshe got up, washed his hands and quietly said the morning brachos. He then whispered to his wife, “Yentel, it’s time for kapparos. Please bring me the notebook from the shelf.”

Moshe sat down on a small stool and lit a candle. The soft light flickered across the room. Yentel brought his notebook, and he began to read through a list of all the aveiros he had done that year.

“One day I spoke lashon hara,” he said in a broken voice. “Another day, I woke up too late to daven. Another time I forgot to give tzedakah.”

As he kept reading, his voice began to shake. Tears filled his eyes and ran down his cheeks. Page after page, Moshe read and cried, until he reached the very end.

After a few moments, he called out again. “Yentel, please bring me the second notebook.”

She dutifully brought it over, and Moshe opened it up.

This notebook was different. Here, Moshe had written down every difficulty that had happened to him over the course of that year.

“One day, the peasants beat me,” he read. “Another day, my child became very sick. In the winter, we had no wood and the inn was freezing cold for many nights. Then our cow died, and we had no milk.”

His voice grew quieter with each word. When he finished, Moshe lifted his eyes toward the sky. “Hashem,” he cried out, “I know I didn’t do everything right. I promised last year that I would be better, but I wasn’t always strong enough.”

He paused, holding the notebook tightly. “But I also trusted You. I asked for a good year, and I believed that You would make that happen.”

The room was very still. “Hashem, today is Erev Yom Kippur,” Moshe continued. “It’s a time when we forgive each other.”

“So I will forgive You, Hashem, for all of the troubles that happened to me. And You, Hashem, in Your kindness, please forgive me, too.”

He then walked over to the fireplace. After one last look at the notebooks, Moshe threw them both into the cackling fire. The notebooks burst into flames, until nothing was left but ashes.

Under the table, the Chassid lay very still. Now he understood why Reb Elimelech of Lizhensk had sent him here. He had finally seen the most special kapparos of all.
