Reb Dovid Leib was working up the courage to confront Reb Sender about his true identity—until he saw something that made him doubt if there was anything special about Reb Sender after all. Reb Dovid Leib noticed Reb Sender spending a lot of time with another unique character in Hatinka: Shmerel.

Shmerel wasn’t good for much.

Shmerel was a man of many nicknames. Some people called him “Shmerel the Aimless Wanderer”. Others, “Shmerel the Stargazer”. A third group way he and his family survived was due to his wife’s hard work spinning wool. And as for his Torah knowledge—it appeared dubbed him “Shmerel the Yawner”. Everyone agreed that that he didn’t even know how to read, based on the way he just stood there silently in shul with his mouth slightly open.

Shmerel was short and bony, with arms and legs that looked like they belonged on a body twice its size. Though Shmerel’s father, Reb Nachum Itzik, was a respected, well- to-do, and quite learned man, none of these qualities seemed to have passed on to his son. Shmerel would spend his days either wandering around the village or perched on an overturned crate in the market square, staring up at the sky with one eye open and one half-closed. He would

often be heard rambling away, sharing his thoughts with anyone who’d listen. One of his favorite subjects to talk about was the clouds.

“The clouds, they’re like people.

They’re always changing. Sometimes the clouds are big and fluffy, other times wispy and gray. Sometimes they look like cows and donkeys, and then they can change into human form. Or the opposite.”

Every other sentence or so, Shmerel’s speech would be interrupted with a yawn. His yawns started out ordinarily enough, but then, as Shmerel opened his thick lips wider and wider, the sound got louder and louder, until it turned into something that sounded like a lion’s roar.

Then Shmerel would pick up where he’d left off:

“Yes, animals into people, people into animals. You know, it reminds me of a story … ”

Children found it entertaining to watch Shmerel, especially when he was yawning. Often, they’d beg Shmerel to yawn again and again, and Shmerel would. Shmerel would yawn until the children begged him to stop, as their stomachs hurt from laughing so much.

Men who passed Shmerel would cluck their tongues and shake their heads.

“A rachmanus on the tatte. A talmid chacham, with a son like this … ”

So, when Reb Dovid Leib saw Reb Sender sitting next to Shmerel, the two of them chatting animatedly as they stared up into the sky, it took him by surprise. If Reb Sender really was a hidden tzaddik, how could he be friends with such a person? Perhaps he was wrong about the red-headed kohen, after all.

Reb Dovid Leib blinked twice and squinted. Who could that be, chanting ma’ariv in such a heartfelt tone, and at this hour?

His eyes widened as he searched for, and found, the source of the noise:

It was coming from a hole in the wall; a makeshift window inside the shack on the corner. The shack’s walls were uneven, one further back than the other. The thatched roof was riddled with holes and overgrown with moss, so that it practically looked like a sukkah. Reb Dovid Leib knew the owner of the shack, and it didn’t fit the voice he was hearing.

It was late one night at the shop; so late that all the streetlamps in Hatinka had burned out. Reb Dovid Leib, who had just finished working, held a candle in his right hand and a bag of shoes in his left as he headed out the door. “Yechaaaaper avon … ”

Reb Dovid Leib walked closer to the hole so he could get a better look at the person singing. Indeed, it was the owner—Shmerel the Stargazer! Shmerel’s eyes scrunched tight and his face was beet red as he continued to chant the words.

“Velooooo yashchis … ”

Reb Dovid Leib walked quietly, his only company being the sound of his feet on the dirt path. First, he took a detour to both the milkman’s cottage and the widow’s home, dropping a pair of shoes for each child by the door.

There was no doubt about it. The man davening this ma’ariv was not, could not, be an ordinary man. This was the tefilla of a tzaddik.
