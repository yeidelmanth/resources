A jug was passed out to each gravedigger, and so the party began. The men quickly got very drunk, singing and dancing together.

Baruch sat as far away as he could from the commotion. He plugged his ears and leaned closer to the table, until his nose was nearly touching the sefer. It was hours before the last of the drunken gravediggers stumbled out the door and Baruch could finally relax. To him, the fear of being alone in such a place was better than the drunken gravediggers’ company.

But the gravediggers didn’t exactly ask Baruch for his opinion. And so, the parties continued at a rate of two or three times a week.

It was called Zalman’s Beis Midrash, after the shamash, Reb Zalman Chayim. Reb Zalman Chayim was called the shamash, but he was really much more than that; he was also the rav, the gabbai, and a true friend to the people of the shul, all at once.

Baruch got to see Reb Zalman Chayim in action on his very first visit to the beis midrash. Just as Baruch was walking in, Reb Zalman Chayim was walking

out, a wooden crate in hand. Reb Zalman Chayim seemed to know everyone; he exchanged nods and smiles with nearly all who passed by. When he reached the middle of Market Street, he turned over his crate and jumped on top of it.

There was something about Reb Zalman Chayim’s manner that

made Baruch stay to watch rather than head inside the beis midrash. For, already in Dobromysl, Baruch had seen that one could learn from observing the right people as much as from a Gemara.

And so, he stood in the doorway of the beis midrash and

watched a ragtag crowd—boys with overgrown hair, girls with twisted braids, dusty beggars, sweaty laborers, and women with toddlers and babies in tow—gather around Reb Zalman Chayim’s crate. Many stall owners up and down the street craned their necks so they, too, could listen from where they were.
