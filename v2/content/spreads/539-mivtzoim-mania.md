“Would you like to shake the lulav and esrog?” Tatty asked. “Well, I don’t know…” mumbled the teenager.

But all of his friends began to cheer him on, “Come on, you can do it! Be a good sport, help these rabbis out!” With encouragement from all of his friends, the young man agreed to shake the lulav and esrog.

Tatty and Shlomo Leib were thrilled to have found someone to do the mitzvah so fast. “I hope the rest of the day goes just like this,” Shlomo Leib said excitedly.

But the further they walked, and the longer they looked, the more it began to seem as if there were no other Jews left in Monticello.

An old woman answered the knocking at the door. “No, I’m not Jewish,” she said. “There used to be a Jewish family living here, but that was many years ago.”

“Do you know if any other Jews live in the neighborhood?” Tatty asked.

“You know,” she replied, “I think there might be someone on the next block. Good luck!”

Tatty and Shlomo Leib quickly ran to the block which the lady had pointed to. They knocked on every door, but did not find any Yidden. They came to the last house, feeling sad that they still hadn’t found another Yid. They were thinking about which route to take back for mincha, when they heard a loud banging.

They looked up and realized that there was a construction project happening right behind the house, and they could see a man sitting atop a huge dump truck. They called up to him, “Hey, how are you? Are you Jewish?”

“You bet I am!” he called back to the shocked bochurim.

“Harry,” was his reply, as he climbed down from the truck.

“Well Dovber, today is the holiday of Sukkos, and we have a very special mitzvah for you to do.”

Right there on the construction site, Dovber shook the lulav and esrog. Then he turned to the bochurim and said, “When I was a boy, I went to synagogue all the time with my grandmother. I loved going with her. She passed away a few years ago, and going to synagogue without her made me feel so sad that I stopped going altogether. And that just made me more sad…Sitting alone every weekend is pretty lonely, you know?

“And that’s when the missionaries knocked on my door. They invited me to come to their church. Of course I told them no way. I’m Jewish, after all! But I was so lonely, and they were so friendly. They continued coming to my house, keeping me company and bringing gifts.

“‘After all,’ I thought to myself, ‘No rabbi ever visits me. I’ll go with them the next time that they ask. What’s the worst that could happen?’
