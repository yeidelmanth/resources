The Alter Rebbe had a wealthy Chassid named Reb Gavriel Nos’ei Chein. After 25 years of marriage, Reb Gavriel and his wife, Chana Rivka, still had no children. Then, sadly, Reb Gavriel had difficulties in business, and lost most of his wealth.

Around that time, the Alter Rebbe was collecting money for Pidyon Sh’vuyim. Reb Gavriel was asked to donate a large amount like he used to give. But Reb Gavriel didn’t have the money. He was devastated.

Chana Rivka immediately sold all her jewelry. There was now money for tzedakah! But first, she polished the coins until they shined brightly, davening that their fortune should also shine. Then, she wrapped up the coins and sent them with her husband.

The mitzvah of Sefiras HaOmer is to count from Pesach to Shavuos.

When we count Sefira, we focus on both the days and the weeks. Do you know how many there are? You got it: There are 49 days; exactly 7 weeks. Now, hold on to those numbers.

Each one of us also has a special connection to 7: our neshama contains 7 middos! Ready for some math? Each midda itself contains 7 parts. So, 7 (middos) x 7 (parts) gets us back to the number 49! 49 days and 49 midda-parts. During each of the 7 weeks, we focus on improving one main midda, and on each of the 49 days, we

focus on a different part of that midda. This way, by the time we reach Shavuos, we’ll have worked on our middos very well—ready to receive the Torah!

The words we say and think when counting Sefira reminds us of these inyanim:

1\) We say the number of the day and week. 2) We say kapitel ס”ז in Tehillim (which has 49 words), thinking of one word each day. 3) We think of one letter from passuk ’ה of that kapitel. (49 letters from the word “יִשְׂמְחּו” until “סֶלָה”) 4) We say the tefillah “ַאָנָא ּבְכֹח”, thinking of one word or roshei teivos. (42 words plus 7 roshei teivos, equals … 49!) 5) We say a special רִּבֹונֹו שֶׁל עֹולָם, mentioning the midda of the day.

You probably know that there are 24 hours in a day, but do you know how many hours there are in a year? The answer is 8,496.

The Rebbe Rashab said that when we “count” our hours and live each one fully and carefully, then the days will also be counted—and not just the days of Sefira. We want to make the most of every precious moment from Hashem. So, when He gives us 8,496 hours each year, we’ll be sure to fill each one with Torah and mitzvos! And, as Chassidim, we’ll see to it that tomorrow will be much better than today.

The days of Sefira are a serious time; the students of Rabi Akiva passed away. For this reason, we don’t do certain things that bring joy:

We don’t say the bracha of ׁשֶהֶחֱיָנּו, except on Shabbos.

We don’t get haircuts (except on Lag BaOmer). Some people have the minhag to get haircuts three days before Shavuos, but minhag Chabad is only to do so on Erev Shavuos. The same is for a boy who turns three during Sefira: His upshern is celebrated either on Lag BaOmer or Erev Shavuos (whichever is on, or after, his birthday).

We also don’t listen to music or make weddings during Sefira, except on Lag BaOmer. We can celebrate engagements, but without music.

Shavuos also has another name: “עֲצֶרֶת”, meaning “stop” or “end”. One reason is because Shavuos is really the end of Pesach. But how?

The Rebbe explains that Pesach is about becoming free. Real freedom doesn’t come from just leaving Mitzrayim. We’re truly free when we keep Torah and mitzvos! So, Pesach’s freedom is only complete with Matan Torah. Today, too, we work hard to become truly free in Avodas Hashem, using these special days of Sefira as proud chayolim do: preparing for Matan Torah! Shining Our Middos

Here’s another connection between Sefira and our middos: Do you know what “סְפִירָה” means? If you said “counting”, you’re right! And if you said “shining”, you’re also right! It means both.

The Maggid of Mezritch taught that working on our middos, especially during Sefira, makes our neshama shine bright.

What time is it right now? There are many right answers. If you’re in Yerushalayim, it’s one time. If you’re in New York, it’s another. In Hawaii, it’s many hours later. What if you keep going west, into the Pacific Ocean? If you go far enough, you’ll reach the International Date Line—the imaginary line that separates days. On one side of the line, it’s one day, and on the other side, it’s a different day. The Rebbe ruled that if someone crosses the Date Line during Sefira, they keep counting Sefira as they were before, even if it’s different from the people around them. So, if someone flies to Australia from America (by going west), they’ll count a different day of Sefira than the people in Australia. The Rebbe recommended not to do that. But, if someone does, they’ll need to celebrate Shavuos either a day earlier or a day later than everyone else, depending on which direction they traveled. For this reason, people who travel to/from Australia for Pesach will often get flights that don’t cross the Date Line, even if it’s a much longer trip.
