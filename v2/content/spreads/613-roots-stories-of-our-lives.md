Mixed reactions could be seen among the cardinals on stage. Some shook their fists in rage, hurling insults at Reb Moshe. Others stayed icily calm, arguing that they were innocent of any wrongdoing. But a few of them looked genuinely ashamed. Their bowed heads and downcast eyes was their silent admission that Reb Moshe was right.

Chairman Domingo stepped back onto the stage. He rang his bell several times until he got a semblance of quiet. “The audience is dismissed,” he

shouted hoarsely, “We will take some time to review the terms of the debate, and will resume at a later time.”

Reb Moshe left the podium with his shoulders straight and his head held high. He nodded politely to the audience members on either side, showing an image of confidence and strength. But inside, Reb Moshe was conflicted. Did I do the right thing? He

On the one hand, Reb Moshe wanted to show an example of a Jew who wasn’t afraid to stand up for himself and his beliefs. On the other hand, the cardinals in the Vatican were very, very powerful men. If Reb Moshe upset them too much, then he, the community in Rome, and Jews all over Europe could be in danger. After all, Reb Moshe had seen how forcefulness with the archbishop had backfired in his own hometown of Posen.

But by now, Reb Moshe was in too deep; there was no way he could back out once he’d started. He could only hope that Hashem would help him navigate the rest of the process, and make a true kiddush Hashem.

When Reb Moshe was nearly at the end of the aisle, a certain young man caught his eye. He was sitting right next to a few prominent Vatican officials and dressed in typical university garb—a green v-shaped doublet, brown breeches, and a cream- colored lace ruff. The young man looked visibly shaken, and was staring at Reb Moshe. There was something about his dark eyes, the shape of his face, and his curly brown hair that made Reb Moshe squint in disbelief. He was almost sure of it: there was a Jew in the Vatican.
