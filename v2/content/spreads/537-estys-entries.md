I guess I should start this journal with a little introduction. When my sister went off to seminary, she left behind a box of all her old stuff and said I can have whatever I want. That’s when I discovered this empty notebook—exactly what I was looking for. You see, one day I’m going to be a five-star general in Tzivos Hashem and everyone will want to know how I got there.

“No need to ask,” I’ll say, “just read my journal, and you, too, will know how to be a five-star general.”

Till then, this journal is TOP SECRET. Permission to read is granted to absolutely no one. Well…unless whoever reads it can help me figure out how to get enough stickers from my apples to cover my Chumash notebook before Mimi does, and how to reach the top of the 40-foot tree in Blueberry Forest, and maybe even help me understand the stuff my teacher teaches—’cause somehow I always manage to get mixed up…

You’ll never guess what my friend Rivky and I are planning.

See, it’s the end of Elul. We’ve had a month when, as my teacher taught us, “מֶלְֶך ּבַׂשָדֶה—the King is in the field.” Hashem comes to visit all of us simple people, and smiles and laughs with us, reminding us that we are all His children.

But now it’s almost Tishrei, when the King goes back to the palace. So Rivky and I are making a goodbye party for Hashem.

Tomorrow she’s gonna come over, and we’re going to bake a seven-layer chocolate cake with blueberries, marshmallows and spicy chips on top. Then we’ll invite all our friends for a big party, and we’ll make goodbye cards for Hashem. I can’t believe no one thought of this before. It makes so much sense.

I have a lot to write about because I’ve been planning my big Rosh Hashanah mission: the mission to bring the mitzvah of shofar to everyone.

See, Mrs. Feigenbaum (our new fourth grade teacher) taught us about what goes on in shul on Rosh Hashana. Turns out, it’s something nobody would want to miss.

Imagine thousands of people dressed in their finest, gathered together with open mouths and trembling knees, craning their necks to see their brand-new king. Royal servants blow trumpets, and a golden crown is placed on top of the king’s head. This is their new ruler; the one person who makes all the country’s rules. Whatever the king decides is immediately carried out. Everybody, even the oldest, the smartest, and the most important people, must listen to him.

That, explained Mrs. Feigenbaum, is what happens on Rosh Hashana!

Except that Hashem is a much greater King than a person-king, which might make you feel like He’s way too up-there to pay attention to us fourth graders down here in Berryville. But no! When we hear the shofar and crown Hashem as King, then Hashem is actually the closest to us than any other time, paying a lot of attention to us.

And that’s why I’m doing Operation Shofar: so no one will miss out on being so close to Hashem at the greatest time of the year.

Every Rosh Hashana, Tatty goes to the retirement home and blows the shofar for people who can’t make it to shul. I asked Tatty if I could come with him this year, and he said yes! Bump-pa-da-dum— Operation Shofar, here we come!

There isn’t going to be a goodbye party after all. But don’t worry—it’s actually a good thing.

You see, we were busy baking our masterpiece when Tatty came in to see what was going on. Like every time Rivky and I bake, things were getting exciting.

After digging out the huge cake mixer, being careful not to use salt instead of sugar like last time, and asking my sister Mushky to check the eggs, we turned on the mixer.

That’s when the bowl started spinning like a dreidel, faster and faster, splashing batter onto the ceiling, until it flew across

the kitchen and out the window. Rivky and I covered our heads with our hands, screaming for help.

Anyway, it’s a good thing Tatty walked by at that moment. He turned off the machine and asked what was going on.

I said that we were just trying to make a goodbye party for Hashem.

And then Tatty told us one pretty important detail: It’s true that the King goes back to the palace—but we go together with Him. And that means we don’t have to say goodbye. In fact, Aseres Yemei Teshuva, the days from Rosh Hashana till Yom Kippur, is a time when Hashem comes the closest ever to us.

Really, all year round Hashem is close to us, like we say in the 12 Pesukim, “'וֽהִנֵה ה נִצָּב עָלָיו—Hashem is standing over us.” He watches proudly, delighted every time we do a mitzvah or learn Torah. But during these ten days, we are even closer to Hashem than usual—right with Him in His palace. All we have to do is realize where we are, and turn to Hashem with more tefillah, Torah and mitzvos, ‘cause that’s how we come closer to Him. It’s like a huge hug, back and forth.
