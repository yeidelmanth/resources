Ezra Yehuda was short but sturdily built, with thick, beefy hands. There was always dirt crusted around his fingernails, and his palms were constantly stained gray. Sometimes the tint was darker, sometimes lighter; but never, as much as he scrubbed them, would the dirt come off entirely. Ezra Yehuda was a gardener by trade, but his unofficial job was to spread important news around town.

Since I began working, I have used every spare moment—five minutes here, five minutes there—to learn Chumash and memorize Tehillim by heart. I wonder how you would have managed such a childhood?”

This speech of Ezra Yehuda’s certainly didn’t calm Zalman Meir; the younger man narrowed his eyes, and exchanged an angry glance with Moshe Leib. One or both of them would have certainly snapped back with something unkind if Eliezer Reuven hadn’t declared that now was the perfect time for him to lead Ezra Yehuda to his garden patch in the back of the smithy, and that he would be so grateful if the gardener could offer any advice for his wilting plants.

But Yitzchak Shaul’s path was different. It reminded Baruch of the ways of Reb Zalman Chayim, Reb Avraham Yitzchak, and the people at the Yutina inn.

It was Friday morning. A line of customers had started to form outside the door of Eliezer Reuven’s smithy even before the sun came up. All the villagers, Jewish or not, knew that Pan Rufke closed his shop at midday on Friday; if they wanted their work done before the weekend, this was their chance. It also didn’t hurt that on Fridays, every customer got a free drink and piece of cake along with their newly-mended horseshoe or plow.

Baruch tried asking Yitzchak Shaul about his background. Where did he study in yeshiva? Who were his Torah teachers? Where did he get his perspective on how to serve Hashem?

Yitzchak Shaul was normally quite open with information. But to Baruch’s surprise, he didn’t say much in answer to Baruch’s questions on this topic. All that he told him was that he was from a small village called Harki, and that he’d studied for a while at a yeshiva in Minsk. He had also learned with the maggid of Harki, Rav Azriel Yosef. When Baruch asked more questions about his primary Torah teacher, or how and why he’d chosen his unique path in Avodas Hashem, Yitzchak Shaul simply shrugged
