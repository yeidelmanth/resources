Observing Reb Zalman Chayim, especially his transformation of Zalman Leib, made a deep impression on Baruch. It taught him that he couldn’t just learn Gemara and halacha, but also sifrei mussar, books that focused on middos and character. He invested his time especially in learning sefarim like Chovos Halevavos of Rabbeinu Bechaye. As Baruch continued his travels,

often not knowing where his next bit of bread would come from, these works of bitachon strengthened his resolve not to accept any help from others and rely only on aid from Above.

Baruch also developed a value for humility and privacy. He tried to find small or out-of-the-way batei midrash, so no one would know about his learning. To the people who hired him, he presented himself as a simple, unlearned orphan looking for work.

For the next three years, this was Baruch’s life: wandering from village to village, from beis midrash to beis midrash, relying on odd jobs here and there to earn his keep. It wasn’t easy. But every time Baruch did find work, he used most of it for bread, and stashed a little bit away for his savings. Every so often, when he managed to save up a significant

sum, he’d settle in one spot for a while, and learn uninterrupted.

It was one of those times when he had enough money saved to last for some time, that seventeen- year-old Baruch arrived in Liozna. Baruch had grown into a tall young man—nearly six feet tall—and a thick blonde beard had sprouted and covered his chin. Despite his lifestyle of hard work and meager meals, Baruch had a naturally strong and well- built figure.

Next, Baruch tried a job as a water carrier. He started knocking on doors, asking people if they needed water delivered to their houses. Most of the people laughed in Baruch’s face; for the poor community in Liozna, having a water carrier was a luxury reserved for the rich.

Baruch then considered chopping wood or catching fish. But he discovered that there were already people doing both of those jobs who were too poor to hire help, and all of whom had large families to feed. Baruch didn’t want to compete with any of these poor men and take away from their meager livelihood.

With no options for work, Baruch began to ration his food. Instead

of one crust of bread in the morning and one in the evening, he ate a half a crust at each meal. Then, he went down to just half

a crust per day. Baruch grew thinner and weaker, and his face got pinched and pale.

As Shavuos neared, Baruch realized that even with this meager diet, at the rate he was going, he wouldn’t have enough money to buy lechem mishneh for the Yom Tov meal. And so, for a few days before Shavuos, Baruch fasted. On Erev Yom Tov, Baruch spent his last two groschen buying food for the seudos.

When Shavuos was over, Baruch had nothing; a period of forced fasting began. He distracted himself from his hunger by taking long walks in the plains of Liozna while reciting Torah from memory. He loved winding through the tall grasses and billowing trees, chanting quietly.

Over the next two days, all Baruch consumed was water from the river. Realizing that he wouldn’t last very long that way, he scavenged around the forests for some edible grasses and herbs. Finally, he found some leaves to eat, and hungrily stuffed them into his mouth. A few minutes later, he collapsed

on the ground. Eating the bitter leaves after a two-day fast had given him a terrible stomach ache.

For hours, Baruch lay on the grass, unable to move. When he finally got up and headed back towards town, he felt quite dizzy, and could hardly walk in a straight line.
