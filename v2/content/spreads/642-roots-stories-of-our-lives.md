Baruch arrived at the Kalisk beis midrash with nothing but a few crusts of bread and a pair of tefillin. By now, he was a good deal thinner than when he’d left Vitebsk, his pants were ragged at the edges, and there was a hole worn into the sole of his right shoe. The months of wandering had begun to take their toll.

Most of the villagers in Kalisk were working men who spent their days out and about tending to business matters. So by

midmorning, the beis midrash was empty—just the way Baruch liked it. He went straight over to the bookshelf, took down a stack of sefarim, and settled himself at the table in the far left corner; the one furthest from the door. Baruch didn’t move from his spot until the room filled up again for mincha.

When Baruch’s bread ran out, he went to the market square in Kalisk and looked around at the stalls, searching for a vendor who looked like he could use a hand. He soon noticed a butcher with a line that stretched until the next stall. The poor man was red and sweaty, struggling to keep up with his customers. Baruch went up to the man and offered to help him for the day in exchange for a few groschen. The butchers gratefully agreed,

and Baruch was grateful to have earned enough money for the next little while. His needs were simple: two dry crusts of bread each day, plus an extra roll and potato for Shabbos.

After a few weeks in Kalisk, Baruch moved on to the next townlet, and then the next. He stayed in each place for just a few weeks or months, supporting himself with odd jobs here and there: chopping wood for an old man, shlepping a peddler’s merchandise, helping a tanner in his shop. In almost every town, there’d be a well-meaning shamash or gabbai—or just a plain townsperson or two—who would try to help Baruch, but Baruch pushed them all away. He wanted to support himself with his own work.
