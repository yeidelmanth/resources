<p class="lead">The amazing story of Krias Yam Suf is recorded in Parshas Beshalach. How did that great neis happen? The Yidden were faced with a frightening challenge—the sea was blocking their way in front and the Mitzrim were chasing them from behind. What were they supposed to do?</p>

Hashem commanded Moshe, “Tell the Yidden to go forward.” Hashem wanted them to go into the sea even before it split.

But only one person was brave enough: Nachshon ben Aminadav. He jumped into the sea with true mesiras nefesh. Only then, when Hashem saw Nachshon fulfilling His command just because He said so, did the neis happen.

With that, the Yidden were rid of the Mitzrim and moved forward to achieve great things: receiving the Torah, building the Mishkan, and entering Eretz Yisroel.

What caused the sea to split?

Waiting until everything felt safe.

Moving forward to do Hashem’s will, even when it was hard.

Speak to Bnei Yisroel, and they should travel forward.

We mention Krias Yam Suf every day in our davening. Why is it so important to remember?

Krias Yam Suf teaches us an important lesson in Avodas Hashem. When it looks like there’s a “sea” of people around us (Jewish or not) who are doing the opposite of what a chayol should do, it can be hard to ignore them and behave the right way. How do we pass this test?

<span class="mc">We can remember what Nachshon did and “jump into the sea” just like him. We can move forward and do what’s right, trusting that Hashem will take care of the rest. When Hashem sees our mesiras nefesh, He will respond just like He did at Krias Yam Suf—He will take away our challenges, and we’ll move forward toward the ultimate goal: Geulah!</span>

“Yankel, please read the next letter,” the melamed said.

Yankel bent over his page. His finger rested on the letter, but his eyes were looking out the window.

The boy blinked. “Uhh … reish?”

The melamed sighed. “It’s a daled.”

He looked around the room. One boy was drawing something in the frost on the window. Another was whispering to the boy next to him. The melamed closed the siddur for a moment and took a deep breath.

These boys weren’t trying to make trouble. They were farm boys. They loved running in the fields, helping their fathers stack the hay, and taking care

The Secret Inside the Trees of the animals. Sitting in cheder was hard for them.

Still, the melamed felt discouraged. If they can barely read the alef-beis, he thought, how will they ever learn Torah?

On Chamisha-Asar B’Shvat, the melamed had a day off. He wrapped himself in his worn coat and walked out of the village toward the forest. How do trees celebrate their Rosh Hashana? he wondered, as the snow crunched under his boots.

<span class="mc" style="color:#1e7dba">He looked at the forest and stopped. The trees were bare. Their branches were covered with thick white snow. They don’t look like they’re celebrating, thought the melamed. They look almost dead.</span>

<span class="mc">Just then, a strong wind blew through the trees. The branches swayed and the snow sparkled in the sunlight. To the melamed, the white snow now looked like Yom Kippur talleisim and kittelach, and the wind sounded like whispered tefillos.</span>

He stood very still and listened. Suddenly, everything changed before his eyes.

The melamed could see through the sparkling snow and through the bumpy bark, as if the trees were made of clear glass.

And deep inside the branches, tiny green sprouts were pushing closer to the outside. They hadn’t come out yet, because the world was still too cold. But no doubt, life was stirring. New buds were waiting for the right time to blossom, when Hashem would send warmth and sunshine.

The melamed stared in wonder. “These trees are not dead,” he whispered. “They are full of life inside.”

A gust of wind blew snow into his face. He closed his eyes. When he opened them again, the forest looked back to normal. The trees were still white and bare; no hints of growth in sight.

But the melamed felt uplifted.

As he walked back to the village, he thought about his students.

I only see the outside, he thought. I see boys who mix up daled and reish. I see boys who draw on the windows instead of focusing. But inside every one of them is a neshama. Inside, every one of them has hidden kochos, waiting to bud and blossom.

The next day, Yankel mixed up the letters again. This time, the melamed didn’t sigh. He bent down gently and pointed.

“Look carefully,” he said with warmth. “A daled has a little corner. Try again.”

Yankel looked at the letter. “Daled,” he said.

The melamed smiled. “Very good.”
