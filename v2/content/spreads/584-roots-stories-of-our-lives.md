Most days were like this, ever since the war started. No one had money to splurge on fresh bread. Many didn’t even have enough for day-old bread, and made do with soups of potato peels and vegetable scraps.

Perel was about to lock up the store for the day when she heard loud thumping at the door.

The door swung open to reveal a tall, well-dressed army officer. Sounds from

the outside—neighing horses, clanging swords, and hoofbeats on the cobblestone—let Perel know that a whole troop of soldiers was waiting outside.

And the order wasn’t finished. Rolls, pastries, jars of spices, bags of flour—the officer stuffed as much as he could into his satchel, and turned to load it onto his wagon outside. Now, all that was left in Perel’s shop was a trail of crumbs and bare wooden shelves.

While the officer was outside, Perel began to dream of what she would do with the payment. The officer’s money would be

enough to feed the whole family for a while. There would even be a little left over for something extra. Maybe she’d buy a fresh

fruit to bring home … or a piece of parchment for Yehuda, so he wouldn’t have to jot his chiddushim1 on the margins of his books. And of course, Perel thought of the new member of their family coming soon as she touched her bulging stomach.
