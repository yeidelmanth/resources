Though Shmerel gave the impression that he was wandering around aimlessly, Reb Dovid Leib began to notice that there was a pattern. Shmerel would always hang around streets where there was someone old, sick, or in need of help. Perhaps a woman who’d just given birth and had no one to help take care of her. Or a lonely old man without any children to visit him. Or a family whose father had broken both of his legs, and was now unable to work.

Shmerel would start by visiting these people and keeping them company, cheering them up with

his chatter. Then, proclaiming that he had “nothing better to do anyways,” he’d help however he could: chop wood, cook a meal, or bring in water from the well.

Seeing this, Reb Dovid Leib was now very sure that Shmerel was a holy man. But identifying the tzaddik was just the first, and easier, step. The harder part would be getting Shmerel to admit his identity, and perhaps even teach him Torah.

Reb Dovid Leib first tried by going up to Shmerel in the marketplace. Shmerel was in his usual position, laid across a couple of wooden

crates, arms folded under his head, his nose pointed to the sky. Reb Dovid Leib walked right up to the crate supporting Shmerel’s head and folded arms. Then he bent down and whispered in his ear: “Reb Shmerel, you’re doing a wonderful job. So much more can be accomplished in secret!”

Reb Dovid Leib straightened, and gave Shmerel a knowing smile.

Shmerel just stared, his mouth hanging slightly open. A bit of spittle dripped from his mouth down onto the floor. Then Shmerel shrugged and looked away.

More often than not, the praise would be followed by words of another sort; the type of comment that would make Avraham Yitzchak’s eyes water and his chest sting. It would be something like, “Sometimes, the apple does fall far from the tree.” Or, “A shame the tatte can’t learn with him, too.”

It would take Avraham Yitzchak all of his efforts to keep quiet and hold back from telling everyone exactly who his tatte was. The hours that his tatte spent learning Kabbalah deep into the night; the notebooks full of his tatte’s handwritten chiddushim; the way his tatte could open practically any sefer to any page, read the first line, and then recite the rest of it from memory.
