Rabbi Hershberg consulted with the Frierdiker Rebbe about becoming the rav for a Lubavitcher shul in Chicago. At that time, the Frierdiker Rebbe sent him to speak with our Rebbe as well.

"I spent almost the entire night with the Rebbe,” he later recalled. “The Rebbe's knowledge and brilliance was astonishing. From that night onward, we maintained a close relationship, and I consulted with the Rebbe about many personal and public matters."

Eventually, Rabbi Hershberg moved to Mexico and became the Chief Rabbi. He established the Beis Din of Latin America, and worked hard to build up Latin Jewry. Because of this, he had many unique opportunities to help Yidden around the world.

In תשל”ט(1979), the American embassy in Iran was seized by a group of Iranian students. 52 people were taken hostage, including six Yidden. In תש”מ (1980), the UN arranged a religious visit for the hostages. Leaders of all religions were allowed to come, including rabbis—but not Americans or Israelis. And so, as the Chief Rabbi of Mexico, Rabbi Hershberg was asked to make the courageous trip.

Of course, it was dangerous for a rabbi to travel to Iran, and Rabbi Hershberg wanted the Rebbe’s bracha.

“Go with Jewish pride,” the Rebbe encouraged Rabbi Hershberg, “You mention that you’ll be there on Chanukah, when the menorah is lit towards the outside. It should be with the strength of “אַל יֵבֹוׁש מִּפְנֵי הַּמַלְעִיגִים”—no shame to be a Yid in front of anyone. Hatzlacha Rabba, and may you bring besuros tovos!”

Deeply uplifted by the Rebbe’s words, Rabbi Hershberg made his way to Iran, proudly armed with his menorah and holy mission.

He developed a relationship with some Iranian officials, and asked if he could light the menorah with the Jewish hostages.

"Just as we have granted permission for a priest to meet with the Christian hostages on their holiday," the Iranians replied, "we will allow you entry as well."

Rabbi Hershberg eagerly brought his menorah to the Yidden in captivity. “It was a very uncommon scene,” he related. “It’s hard to describe their excitement when they saw me and the menorah. After lighting the candles, we sang and danced together—in the middle of Iran! They were full of joy.”

The next day, Rabbi Hershberg and the other religious leaders were invited to join a massive prayer service attended by Khomeini, the ruler of Iran,

and thousands of his people. Rabbi Hershberg and his colleagues were prominently seated in the front row.

At a certain point in the prayers, everyone in the audience bowed to the ground. All of the visiting religious leaders did the same. All, except for Rabbi Hershberg.

Khomeini sent a messenger to Rabbi Hershberg demanding an explanation. “How dare you not bow during the public service?” he was asked.

With the help of a translator, Rabbi Hershberg calmly responded, “I am a Jew, and I don’t speak your language. How could I bow, without knowing who you are bowing to?”

Of course, the incident created an enormous Kiddush Hashem. Rabbi Hershberg used the incredible opportunity to arrange an official meeting with the ruler himself, and was able to help the Iranian Yidden in many ways.

Even in the darkest of places, our Yiddishe pride can shine bright. With the Rebbe’s brachos, Rabbi Hershberg openly lit the menorah for Yidden in captivity, and stood without fear before the highest Iranian officials.

We, too, must fulfill our mission loud and proud. We light public menorahs for all to see, and won’t stop until every last Yid is reached.

So grab your menorahs, light up that Yiddishe pride, and hit the streets.
