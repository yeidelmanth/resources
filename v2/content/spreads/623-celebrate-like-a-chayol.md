It was the year תד”ש )1944(, during World War Two. Things were finally looking better—America had just conquered Italy from the Nazis, yimach shemam! But they still had much to do, and so the war raged on.

At this time, the Frierdiker Rebbe wrote a letter to the American government, asking them to get him a Calabria esrog for Sukkos. The Frierdiker Rebbe understood very well that the government was extremely busy—especially while at war!—and only asked because it was so important.

What a powerful lesson we can learn from this story! Using Calabria esrogim is not just a “nice thing”, but truly a necessary minhag.

It’s time to celebrate the Yom Tov of Sukkos! Of course, there are many minhagim for this joyous time. We use a special type of esrog and perform the mitzvah of “living

in the sukkah” in our own way. Every night of Sukkos, we are visited by Chassidishe Ushpizin and celebrate Simchas Beis Hasho’eiva. There are even minhagim for

bentching lulav and esrog. Let’s learn all about them. Calabria

Hashem first gave the mitzvah of arba minim when the Yidden were in the midbar. So, Moshe Rabbeinu sent messengers to find the best esrogim. After traveling around, they found the most mehudar esrogim in Calabria, an area in Italy.

Since that’s where Moshe Rabbeinu’s esrogim came from, Chabad chassidim are careful to use Calabria esrogim, as well (also called “Yanover esrogim”, the name of the port city).

As you walk into a sukkah, you could probably tell right away if it’s a Chabad sukkah or not. Just look around!

You see, many Yidden work hard to decorate their sukkahs with all sorts of beautiful decorations. Minhag Chabad, though, is not to decorate our sukkah. The Rebbe writes in a letter that it’s because the sukkah walls themselves are special. We want to notice and appreciate their kedusha and beauty without covering them over with decorations.

Additionally, the Baal Shem Tov said that his sukkah decorations were the Yidden who stepped inside. With their emunah and kabbalas ol, they brought joy to Shamayim and made his sukkah beautiful.

So, this year, think about the beauty of your special sukkah—complete with plain walls and many precious Yidden!

Although many Yidden have the custom to sleep in the sukkah, Chabad chassidim specifically don’t sleep in the sukkah.

We have this minhag because the sukkah is too holy for sleeping! Once, the Mitteler Rebbe was told that some of the younger chassidim were sleeping in the sukkah. The Mitteler Rebbe was shocked. “How can they sleep in the sukkah? The energy of ‘makif d’bina’ (a very high level of Elokus) is there!

“When a person is awake, he is in control of himself; but while sleeping, he’s not. So how can someone be in a place of makif d’bina when he’s not in control?”

As chayolim, we are mekushar to the Rebbe. If the Rebbe feels the great holiness of the sukkah and therefore cannot allow himself to sleep there, we chassidim don’t sleep there, either.

When was the happiest time in the Beis Hamikdash? Simchas Beis Hasho’eiva! Throughout the year, when the daily karbanos were brought, the kohanim poured wine on the Mizbei’ach. But on Sukkos, they also poured water. And getting (or “drawing”) this water was done with tremendous joy. Each night of Sukkos, before drawing the water, the Yidden held massive celebrations. Huge torches were lit, Levi’im played happy music, and great chachamim juggled fire. Everybody danced all night long. These celebrations are called “ׂשִמְחַת ּבֵית הָהַׁשֹואֵב—The Celebration of the House of Drawing \[Water\].” But when the Beis Hamikdash was destroyed, Simchas Beis Hasho’eiva was not what it used to be. That is, until the Rebbe reintroduced it with a shturem! On Sukkos תשמ”א )1980(, the Rebbe explained that even today, Simchas Beis Hasho’eiva is a time for great joy. We should be so freilech that our joy “spills out onto the streets”! That night, chassidim danced in the streets of Crown Heights. The same thing happened the next night. And the next! Since then, Chabad chassidim have danced every night of Sukkos, and thousands of others join in—just like the Yidden in the Beis Hamikdash!

Although we don’t sleep in the sukkah, we spend every possible moment there— even when we don’t have to. According to halacha, only important foods and drinks must be eaten in the sukkah, while snacks and water can be enjoyed anywhere. But our minhag is to be extra strict: Men and boys don’t eat or drink anything outside the sukkah—no matter the weather.

Rain or shine, and even if it’s pouring buckets outside, Chabad chassidim only eat in the sukkah. We do the same on Shmini Atzeres, when eating in the sukkah is a minhag, not a mitzvah.

The Sukkah is so holy that we can’t imagine eating anywhere else, even if it means getting soaked! Arba Minim

Picking up a lulav and esrog is not so simple. You might do the mitzvah without realizing it—just by holding them together! See, the mitzvah is not to shake the lulav and esrog, but to hold them together. So, if someone has already held them together, they can no longer make the bracha that day. We want to say the bracha each time we do the mitzvah, so we pick them up carefully. First, we lift the lulav in our main (right) hand and say the bracha, “עַל נְטִילַת לּולָב”. Then, with the other hand, we lift the esrog. On the first day of Sukkos, we keep them apart a bit longer and say “ׁשֶהֶחֱי ָנּו”. Then, we put them together and shake three times in each direction. Right, left, forward; up, down, backwards. Our minhag is to keep the lulav upright and bring the arba minim to our heart after each shake. We also make sure to share our arba minim with others. The Rebbe’s esrog would turn brown from so many chassidim using it, and this brought the Rebbe much joy. “There is no esrog more beautiful than one used by many Yidden,” he wrote. This year, let’s get out there and share our esrogim with as many Yidden as possible. Onwards to mivtzoim!

It was pouring rain one Sukkos in Lubavitch, and the Temimim went to eat in the sukkah anyway. When the Rebbe Rashab was told that they were sitting in the sukkah during the downpour, putting their jackets over their heads for protection, he happily noted, “The spirit of Tomchei Temimim has affected them.”
