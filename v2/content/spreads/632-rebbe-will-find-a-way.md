She felt very nervous. Maybe I shouldn't run dance classes after all.

To distract herself, she clicked on someone’s status. And then—her heart skipped a beat.

It was a picture of the Rebbe giving a dollar to a woman, Mrs. Taibel Lipskier. And under the picture were the words: “Dance your fears away.”

Mommy was amazed. It was exactly the message she needed to hear, at exactly the right moment.

She posted the flyer. And guess what? People signed up! Soon, she was teaching all types of Jewish women and girls how to dance to niggunim!

Later on, Mommy found out more about the story behind the picture. Mrs. Taibel Lipskier had asked the Rebbe what to do about her anxious feelings. The Rebbe told her, “Go to as many weddings as possible and dance, and inspire other people to dance, too.”

bring joy to others, he had sent Mommy the very same

Mommy’s fingers hovered over the “send” button. But she was too afraid to press it.

Ever since she was a little girl, Mommy loved to dance. She joined all kinds of dance classes—ballet, tap, jazz, and more. She even joined competitions, took tests, and learned how to teach dance classes herself.

Then, Mommy started learning Torah. As she studied the halachos of tznius, she realized she couldn’t go to her regular dance classes anymore. It was hard to give up dancing, but she knew it was the right thing to do.

After she got married and had children, people would sometimes ask, “Can you teach my daughter to dance?” But Mommy always said no. She didn’t feel comfortable dancing to non-Jewish music anymore. She only wanted to listen to niggunim. But she coudn’t find any recordings that would work for a dance class.

Still, Mommy missed dancing. She dreamed of teaching dance again—but how?

One day, while teaching at a school in Monsey, the principal came over to her. “I have some niggunim played on piano,” she said. “Can you choose one and make a dance for the Mother-Daughter Tea?”

When listening to the tracks, Mommy was surprised— and excited! She had never

known that there were such beautiful recordings of niggunim. “Wow,” she thought. “You can dance to niggunim!”

She began collecting niggunim recorded with instruments like piano, violin, and percussion—beautiful music that you could dance to. She hoped that maybe one day, she’d teach frum girls how to dance in a holy and tzniusdik way.

But there was one problem: Our family lived in a small apartment, and there was no space for dance classes.

Later, when our family moved to Baltimore and bought a house, Mommy was so happy. Now there was finally room for a dance studio! She fixed up the basement with smooth flooring and ballet barres, and was ready to start giving classes.

She had a flyer made and opened her WhatsApp to send it out. But suddenly… she froze.

I know how to dance, and I know how to teach dance classes—but how will I run a business? What if no one signs up? What if people don’t want to pay?
