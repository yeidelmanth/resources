Inside the satchel there were the few objects that she’d managed to gather before she left: a spare kerchief, a hunk of bread, a siddur, and a silver pendant—the one which her husband Moshe had made for her when they first got married.

Sara could still hear the rioting crowds in the city streets behind her, and the smell of burning wood filled the air. Years of the archbishop’s fiery anti-Jewish speeches were coming to the fore in the ugliest of ways. Sara’s non-Jewish neighbors—the

milkman to whom she would nod “Good morning” each day, the fruit-seller from whom she bought produce in the weekly marketplace, and the teenage boy who occasionally lit the fire for her on Shabbos—were all on a wild pogrom, burning and destroying all Jewish property in their path.

Sara counted heads. Freida, Kaddish, Rochel, Shneur Zalman, Yehuda, Mordechai … baruch Hashem. Everyone was there.

Next, Sara patted her apron. She breathed a sigh of relief as she felt the bulges at the seams. The day her husband left for Rome, she sewed gold coins and gems into the hem with a heavy heart, hoping she would never have to

make use of them. But now, Sara was glad she’d done so. In the rush of fleeing from the pogrom, there was no time to gather anything valuable at all.

Reb Moshe’s heart sank to his stomach as he read his wife’s letter. As soon as he had finished reading, he stuffed the paper in his pocket, and packed his bags. He frantically scribbled a note to explain where he had gone and left it with the hotel clerk; there would be no time for a proper goodbye. Then, his heart racing, he ran as fast his feet could carry him. He needed to catch the first stagecoach out of London.

Reb Moshe and his family were welcomed warmly by the Prague community; as descendants of the Maharal, they were considered royalty. And Reb Moshe’s grandfather, Reb Shmuel, was still remembered by the eldest members of the community as a Rosh Hakahal who led with kindness and wisdom.

But still, Reb Moshe couldn’t bear to stay too long in Prague. He didn’t want to remain a refugee, a wandering guest, or a survivor of tragedy. After just a few weeks, Reb Moshe started to travel again—both for business reasons and to scout out a new, permanent home for his family. Reb Moshe soon settled on two choices: the cities of Cracow and Minsk.

The community of Cracow stood out to Reb Moshe—much like it had stood out to his great- great-grandfather, the Maharal, years before—because of the great value which they placed on Torah. Even the working-class people were meticulous in setting times for Torah learning and in their mitzvah observance. And everyone, whether learned

or simple, aimed to marry their daughter to a talmid chacham.

Minsk was also a city that was rich in Torah study. However, the yeshivos there were not as prominent as the ones in Cracow. Additionally, there were no people who, like Reb Moshe,

Posen, Tatte,” he said. “I’ve decided to go to Vitebsk, where most of them have settled.”

“And you’ll have enough money there?” Reb Moshe raised an eyebrow. From the beginning, he never approved of Shneur Zalman having a job that paid so little. But in Minsk, at least, he’d be able to check on his son and support him if needed.

Reb Moshe sighed. He knew from experience that his son had a stubborn streak. “If you ever change your mind, you’ll have space with us.”

As Reb Moshe suspected, Shneur Zalman didn’t change his mind.

And so, a few weeks later, Shneur Zalman and Rochel set out for Vitebsk. Kaddish and Freida decided that they would spend a short time with Reb Moshe and Sara in Minsk, and then go join them; Kaddish was very close with Shneur Zalman, and the two still learned regularly.

It put Reb Moshe at ease to know that Kaddish would move to Vitebsk as well. His gifted son-in-law had never regained his Torah scholarship, but did become fairly successful in business. So Kaddish and Shneur Zalman would be able to help

one another: Kaddish could supplement Shneur Zalman’s meager teacher’s salary, if necessary, and Shneur Zalman could continue learning Torah with Kaddish.

Shabbos after Reb Moshe arrived, he gave a d’var Torah in the main shul. A large group of talmidei chachamim stayed for hours after the speech, asking Reb Moshe their biggest questions in Torah learning.
