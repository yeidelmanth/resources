Erev Yom Kippur was a very serious time for the Rebbe Rashab. He would wake up early and finish his seudah before 12:00 p.m., so he could say extra Tehillim.

The year תרמ”ח or תרמ”ט (1887 or 1888) was like any other. As usual, the Rebbe Rashab woke up early, and was soon sitting with a sefer in his study. Then, there was a knock at the door, and in walked the chossid Reb Zalman from Cherbin. He boomed out in a cheerful voice, “Rebbe! Gut Yom Tov!”

The Rebbe Rashab replied solemnly, “It is true that today is a Yom Tov, but we must use the day to do teshuva and make hachlatos. It is a serious time.”

Reb Zalman responded, “Rebbe, we are soldiers. Erev Yom Kippur is a Yom Tov, and we are commanded by Hashem to be happy on Yom Tov. So, as soldiers of Hashem, our mission today is to do our avodah with joy.

The Rebbe Rashab was very happy with Reb Zalman’s answer, and handed him a piece of lekach.

The holiest time of the year is coming! As chayolim, Erev Yom Kippur and Yom Kippur are full of special minhagim.

Erev Yom Kippur starts early with kaparos. Then, we ask for lekach and receive brachos from our parents. On Yom

Kippur itself, we completely skip the bracha of ”“ׁשֶעָשָׂה לִי ּכָל צָרְּכִי and sing a special niggun right before the

end of the fast. Let’s learn about these minhagei Chabad!

Erev Yom Kippur starts early in the morning with kaparos. (This minhag can also be done throughout Aseres Yemei Teshuva.) Boys take a male chicken and girls take a female chicken. We wave the chicken over our heads, and say the tefillah of “זֶה חֲלִיפָתִי” for males, and “זֹאת חֲלִיפָתִי” for females. Then, the chicken is shechted and given to the poor. We hope that by giving it to tzedakah, it will help to bring us a kapara for our aveiros.

Kaparos is a minhag done with seriousness and care. The Rebbe would hold his chicken very gently and with both hands, so as not to hurt it, chas v’shalom.

Kaparos is also a chance to do a mitzvah that doesn’t come up very often, called “ּכִיסּוי הַדָם”. We take some dirt, cover the blood of the chicken that was shechted, and make a special bracha.

What’s the sweetest way to prepare for Yom Kippur? With lekach (honey cake), of course!

Our minhag is to ask someone on Erev Yom Kippur to give us lekach. The Rebbe himself would stand and give out lekach for hours on this holy day. It’s explained that the person giving out lekach is a shliach of Hashem. This means that the lekach doesn’t just come from them, but from Hashem Himself!

Do you know why we are davka supposed to ask for lekach, and not just take it? That’s because this minhag of asking for lekach isn’t just about getting honey cake. It’s for a very deep reason: If Hashem decreed that we are supposed to beg for tzedakah this year, we want the request for lekach on Erev Yom Kippur to fulfill this gezeira, leaving us with no need to ask or beg for anything else in the coming year.

When the Baal Shem Tov would give out lekach, he would say: “I am giving you lekach, and Hashem should give you a good, sweet year!”

Erev Yom Kippur is also a time when fathers give brachos to their children, known as “Birchas Habanim”. And so, each year before Kol Nidrei, the Rebbe would give these special brachos to his children: the Temimim.

This minhag began in תשי”ב )1951(, the first Yom Kippur of the Rebbe’s Nesius. The Rebbe asked that the bochurim gather in his room and told them, “Since you are learning in the Rebbe’s yeshiva, you are the Rebbe’s children!” The Rebbe bentched them with the regular Birchas Habanim, and added a bracha for hatzlacha in their learning with Yiras Shamayim.

We, as chayolim of Tzivos Hashem, are also the Rebbe’s children. We, too, surely receive these special brachos and special kochos to make the Rebbe proud!

One of the happiest moments of Yom Kippur is at the end of the day, during the last kaddish of Ne’ila. Right before we blow the shofar to signal the end of Yom Kippur, our minhag is to sing a special niggun called “Napoleon’s March”.

Napoleon’s March is a tune that the French Army used to sing as they marched off to battle. When the Alter Rebbe heard the tune, he said: “This is a niggun of victory!” Since then, it has become a Chabad niggun.

In תרנ”ט (1898), the Rebbe Rashab asked to sing this niggun during Ne’ila. From then on, it has become our minhag to do so every year. It is always sung with great chayus and joy. In the early years, the Rebbe would jump onto his chair to encourage the singing! People from shuls all around Crown Heights would come to 770 for Ne’ila so they could experience the powerful moment of Napoleon’s March.

After such a serious day, why do we immediately switch to such simcha? It’s because we are confident that our Yom Kippur avodah was successful, and we are all going to have a good year. So of course, we sing and dance with the greatest joy as we get ready for the second part of Tishrei: Z’man Simchaseinu!

There is a bracha in birchos hashachar that we skip on Yom Kippur. Do you know which one it is?
